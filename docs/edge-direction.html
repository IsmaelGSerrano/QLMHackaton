Which way does the money flow?

A visual guide to the "edge direction" decision: why the usual quantum graph circuit cannot see which account paid which, and what we have to choose to fix it.

accounttransaction (payer → payee)
Step 1 · The data

AML data is a picture of who paid whom

AML means Anti-Money Laundering. Money laundering is moving criminal money through many accounts until it looks legitimate. Our dataset (IBM's synthetic AML transactions, HI-Small) is a long list of bank transfers, and a few of them are labelled as laundering, about 1 in 1,000.

Draw each account as a dot and each transaction as an arrow from the payer to the payee, and you get a graph like the one above. The arrow has a direction: "A paid B" is a different fact from "B paid A".

Step 2 · What laundering looks like

Laundering leaves shapes in the graph

The dataset's pattern file lists 370 laundering attempts, each one a small shape of 8 types. Look at the two outlined ones: FAN-OUT and FAN-IN have exactly the same shape. The only difference is the direction of the arrows.

Step 3 · Graph to quantum circuit

Each account becomes a qubit, each payment becomes a gate

Our plan (Option B, after Skolik et al.) builds the circuit directly from the subgraph. Every account gets one qubit, drawn as a horizontal wire. Every transaction becomes a two-qubit gate that connects the wires of the two accounts. The circuit's shape is the graph's shape.

→

Every wire starts in the same small rotation (in the real model this is where account features go). Then the gates run, then we measure each qubit. The pattern of measured values is what the classifier sees.

Step 4 · The problem, live

Try it: can the circuit tell the two shapes apart?

Pick a pair of shapes and a gate. The page simulates both circuits for real (a small state-vector simulator runs in your browser) and compares the outputs. With the usual gate RZZ, the two outputs are always identical, whatever the angle. Switch to a directed gate and they split.

Why RZZ fails

A symmetric gate erases the arrow

RZZ (and CZ) treat both qubits the same way: swap the two wires and you get the same gate. So "A pays B" and "B pays A" put the same gate in the circuit. Flip every arrow in a fan-out and you get a fan-in, but the circuit has not changed at all. It is not that the model fails to learn the difference; the difference never reaches the model.

A directed gate has two different ends. In ZX the payer gets a Z and the payee gets an X. In CRY the payer is the control and the payee gets rotated. Reverse the arrow and you get a different gate, so different shapes give different outputs.

Step 5 · The catch: gate order

Directed gates care about which one runs first

RZZ gates can run in any order and give the same result. Directed gates cannot, when one account both receives and pays, which is exactly what a laundering middle-man does. Below, B receives from A and pays C. Swap the order of the two gates and the output changes.

So we need a fixed rule for the order, and the rule must not depend on how we named the accounts. Use the transaction timestamp. Money moves in time, so the circuit applies the gates in the order the money actually moved.

Step 6 · Amount, time and repeated payments

What sets the angle, and what to do with repeat payments

Each gate has an angle θ. We set it from the transaction, for example θ = w · log(amount). The weight w is learned during training and is the same for every edge: that keeps the circuit fair to every account, so renaming accounts does not change the answer.

Two accounts often have several transactions between them (the graph is a multigraph). We have to pick one of two ways to handle that:

Merge them recommended

  • One gate per pair of accounts, angle from the total amount.
  • The count of payments goes in as an extra feature.
  • Shallow circuit: 2 CZ per pair on Garnet.

One gate per payment more detail, more noise

  • Keeps each amount and timestamp.
  • 3 payments cost 6 CZ instead of 2.
  • Every extra CZ adds noise on real hardware.
The decision

The pitch for the judges: laundering is a directed shape in time; our circuit is built from that shape, with direction, time and amount inside the gates.
